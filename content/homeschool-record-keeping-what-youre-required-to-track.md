# Homeschool Record Keeping: What You're Required to Track

**Good homeschool records protect your family, support your child''s future opportunities, and — often overlooked — give you a meaningful picture of how far your child has come. ## Why Record Keeping Matters Beyond Compliance {#why-it-matters}

Many homeschooling families approach record keeping as a bureaucratic obligation — something to satisfy the school board or state authority. That''s an understandable perspective, but it misses the most valuable aspect of good records.

**Records tell the story of your child''s education.** Years from now, when your teenager is applying to college or a competitive program, the documentation you kept from their elementary years will form the foundation of their academic story. A well-maintained learning log is also a profound gift to your child: evidence that their education was intentional, comprehensive, and tailored to them.

**Records protect your family.** In most jurisdictions, homeschooling operates under varying degrees of oversight. Documentation that you''ve been meeting attendance requirements and covering core subjects is your best defence against any challenge to your homeschool''s legitimacy.

**Records help you plan better.** Reviewing a term''s worth of daily logs reveals patterns: what subjects your child thrives in, what''s being neglected, what teaching approaches work. Records are a planning tool, not just an archive.

## The Legal Landscape: Requirements Vary Significantly {#legal-landscape}

This is the most critical thing to understand about homeschool record keeping: **there is no single answer.** Requirements vary dramatically by country, state, and sometimes even local jurisdiction.

### United States
The US has the widest range of state requirements:
- **No regulation states** (Texas, Oklahoma, Alaska, others): No notification, no required records, no assessments
- **Low regulation states** (most): Parents notify the school district; attendance and subject coverage records recommended
- **Moderate regulation states**: May require annual assessment or portfolio review
- **Higher regulation states** (New York, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, others): Specific annual notification requirements, required subject lists, mandatory assessments or portfolio reviews by a qualified evaluator

[NEEDS VERIFICATION: Current state-by-state regulation status as of 2026 — regulations change. Readers should verify via HSLDA (Homeschool Legal Defense Association) or their state homeschool association for current requirements.]

### Australia
Each state and territory has its own registration and reporting requirements. Most require initial registration with the relevant education authority and annual reporting demonstrating educational progress. The Northern Territory, Victoria, NSW, Queensland, WA, SA, Tasmania, and ACT each have distinct processes.

[NEEDS VERIFICATION: Current state-by-state requirements in Australia as of 2026.]

### United Kingdom
Home education in England, Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland has varying requirements. In England, there is no legal requirement to register (unless the child was previously enrolled in a school that deregistered them), but local authorities may request evidence of education.

### The Universal Principle
**Verify your specific local requirements before designing your record keeping system.** The HSLDA (US), your state homeschool association, or equivalent national body in your country is the most reliable source of current, jurisdiction-specific requirements.

## The Essential Records: What Most Families Should Keep {#essential-records}

![An infographic showing the essential homeschool records.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-infographic.jpg)
*A visual checklist of the essential homeschool records.*

Regardless of your local requirements, these are the records that will serve your family across all scenarios — compliance, college applications, school re-entry, and your own planning:

1. **Attendance/school days log** — dates and hours of learning
2. **Daily learning log** — what was covered each day
3. **Portfolio of student work** — samples demonstrating learning progress
4. **Curriculum list** — what materials, programs, and resources you''ve used
5. **Assessment records** — any tests, evaluations, or formal assessments
6. **High school transcript** (for secondary students) — course names, grades, credit hours

Everything else is supplementary. Get these six right and you''re in excellent shape for almost any requirement or situation.

## Attendance Records: How to Track and What to Include {#attendance}

Attendance records serve a specific legal function: demonstrating that your child received instruction for the required number of days (or hours) in a given academic year.

![A close-up of a homeschool attendance log.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-attendance.jpg)
*A simple paper attendance log — date, subjects, hours, and brief notes — meets most jurisdictional requirements.*

### What to Record
- **Date** of instruction
- **Total hours** of learning (or a notation of full day/half day)
- **Subjects covered** (even a brief list: maths, reading, science project)
- **Location** (home, library, field trip, co-op class — if your jurisdiction counts varied settings)

### What Counts as a School Day
Most jurisdictions define a school day as a minimum number of instructional hours — commonly 4–6 hours. Homeschool advocates note that homeschool instruction is often more efficient per hour than classroom instruction; many families comfortably meet annual day requirements in significantly less total time.

**Field trips, educational outings, co-op classes, and library visits typically count.** Document them as you would any school day.

### Attendance Tracking Tools
- **Paper log:** A simple notebook with a daily entry — date, subjects, hours. Effective and portable.
- **Spreadsheet:** Date, subject columns, hours total. Easy to calculate annual totals.
- **Printed planner:** Homeschool planners designed for this purpose with daily log pages
- **App/software:** Dedicated homeschool planning apps that auto-calculate attendance totals

## Daily Learning Logs: Your Most Valuable Record {#daily-logs}

If you only keep one record beyond an attendance tally, make it a **daily learning log**. This is where attendance documentation and genuine educational record merge.

![A close-up of an open daily learning log.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-daily-log.jpg)
*A descriptive daily log is a complete educational record.*

### What a Good Daily Log Includes
- **Date**
- **Subject areas covered** with brief notes (not just "maths" — "worked on multiplication tables 6–9, used Cuisenaire rods, completed worksheet")
- **Books read or read together** — title, pages covered or book completed
- **Activities and projects** — science experiment, art project, nature walk, cooking project
- **Notable moments** — "finally understood long division," "wanted to keep reading past bedtime"
- **Hours** or time periods

### Why the Detail Matters
The brief descriptions you write today become the evidence of learning you need tomorrow — for a portfolio review, a college application, or simply for yourself when you can''t remember what you covered in Year 5. A sparse log ("did maths, did reading") is nearly useless in retrospect. A descriptive log is a complete educational record.

### Keeping It Manageable
Most experienced homeschool parents spend 5–10 minutes at the end of each school day on their log. The key is making it part of your daily closing routine, not a separate administrative task.

## Building a Homeschool Portfolio {#portfolio}

A portfolio is a curated collection of your child''s work that demonstrates learning and growth over time. Some jurisdictions require annual portfolio reviews; others don''t. Regardless, portfolios are immensely valuable.

![A parent and child looking through a homeschool portfolio binder.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-portfolio.jpg)
*A subject-tabbed portfolio binder with work samples across subjects — both required and voluntary documentation.*

### What to Include in a Homeschool Portfolio

**Writing samples:** Several pieces from across the year, ideally including drafts that show revision and improvement. Short stories, essays, research summaries, journal entries.

**Maths work:** Completed worksheets, problem sets, or projects. Include work that shows progression — some from early in the year and some from later.

**Science and social studies projects:** Photos of experiments, written summaries, diagrams, research projects.

**Reading log:** List of books read during the year, with brief responses if available.

**Art and creative projects:** Drawings, craft projects, photographs of 3D work.

**Special projects:** The homeschool advantage — field trips, deep-dive studies, real-world learning that a portfolio can capture and a transcript cannot.

### Portfolio Organisation
Organise chronologically or by subject — whichever makes sense for your jurisdiction''s review requirements or your own filing preferences. A tabbed three-ring binder per year is a classic format. Digital portfolios (scanned work + photos) work equally well.

## Academic Transcripts: When and How to Create Them {#transcripts}

For elementary and middle school students, a formal transcript is rarely necessary. For high school students, it becomes critically important.

![A mock-up of a professional-looking homeschool high school transcript.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-transcript.jpg)
*A homeschool transcript is a key document for college applications and future employment.*

### What a Homeschool Transcript Includes
- **Student name and contact information**
- **School name** (your homeschool — you can give it a formal name)
- **Years of study**
- **Course list** by year with course names (e.g., "English Literature 11," "Algebra I," "World History")
- **Grades or performance descriptors** (letter grades, percentages, or narrative assessment)
- **Credit hours** (typically Carnegie units in the US: 1 credit = approximately 120–150 hours of instruction)
- **Cumulative GPA** (if using grades)
- **Date of graduation/completion**

### Creating Credit Hours
Even without formal classes, you can calculate credit hours from your daily log. 120–150 hours of instruction in a subject = 1 credit. Your daily logs make this calculation possible.

### Homeschool Transcript Resources
Several templates and resources exist for creating homeschool transcripts. HSLDA and many homeschool co-ops offer transcript templates. The key is consistency across the high school years — establish your grading/assessment system in Year 9 and maintain it.

## Curriculum and Resource Records {#curriculum-records}

Keeping a list of the curriculum materials, programs, books, and resources you''ve used serves several purposes:

- Evidence that you''ve covered required subjects
- A reference for portfolio reviews or assessments
- Documentation for college applications ("used Teaching Textbooks for Algebra" or "enrolled in dual enrollment at community college")
- Your own reference for planning future years

**What to record:** Program or curriculum name, publisher, year used, grade level, subjects covered. A simple spreadsheet works well.

---

## Assessment Records {#assessment-records}

Depending on your jurisdiction, you may be required to document annual assessments. Even where not required, assessment records have value.

### Types of Assessment Evidence
- **Standardised test results** (if you administer them): CAT, IOWA, Stanford, or other norm-referenced tests
- **Portfolio reviews** (if your state requires review by a qualified evaluator): retain the evaluator''s written feedback
- **Co-op or outside class grades**
- **Online course completions and certificates**
- **Informal assessments** you''ve conducted: reading level checks, maths benchmarks

Assessment records demonstrate progress over time — important for your planning and for external verification of educational adequacy.

## Organising Your Records: Systems That Work {#organising}

![A shot of a well-organized shelf with labeled binders for each school year.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-organization.jpg)
*A multi-year record keeping system — annual binders plus digital backup provides complete documentation.*

### The Annual Binder System
One binder per child per year, divided into:
- Attendance log
- Daily learning log (or summaries)
- Portfolio work samples
- Curriculum list
- Any assessment documentation

Simple, contained, and easy to access years later.

### The Digital System
Scan or photograph work samples. Use Google Drive, Dropbox, or a dedicated app. Maintain a spreadsheet as your daily log. Back up to two locations (cloud + external drive).

**Advantage:** Easy to search, share with evaluators, and never physically lost.
**Disadvantage:** Requires discipline to scan regularly; photos of work need reasonable quality.

### The Hybrid System
Physical daily log (easier to write in the moment), digital photos of portfolio pieces, digital spreadsheet for attendance summary. Many families find this the most sustainable combination.

## Digital vs Physical Records {#digital-vs-physical}

![A split-screen image showing a physical planner on one side and a digital record-keeping app on a tablet on the other.](/images/articles/homeschool-record-keeping-guide-digital-vs-physical.jpg)
*Both digital and physical record-keeping systems can be effective.*

Both work. The best system is the one you''ll actually maintain.

**Physical records:** More tactile and visible, harder to lose for tech-averse families, the "portfolio binder" has a satisfying tangibility that digital doesn''t. Vulnerable to fire, flood, and physical damage — always photograph or scan important records.

**Digital records:** Searchable, easy to share, easy to back up across multiple locations, space-efficient, allows photos of 3D projects and activities. Requires consistent backup habits to be reliable.

**Recommendation:** Maintain your daily log and attendance record in whatever form you''ll actually keep up with daily (most families find paper easier for in-the-moment logging). Digitise portfolio pieces and important documents for backup.

## How Long to Keep Records {#how-long}

- **Annual attendance and daily logs:** Minimum 7 years, or until adulthood
- **Portfolio samples:** Minimum through childhood; consider permanent digital archive
- **High school transcripts:** Permanently — this is your child''s official academic record
- **Assessment records:** 7 years minimum, or as required by jurisdiction
- **Curriculum lists:** 7 years minimum

Given the near-zero cost of digital storage, many families simply keep all digital records permanently. For physical records, photograph important items before discarding.

## Record Keeping for Re-Entry to Conventional School {#re-entry}

If your child may transition to a conventional school at any point, records become particularly important. Schools typically require:

- **Documentation of grade level and academic progress** — daily logs and portfolio work demonstrate this
- **Immunisation records** (not academic, but worth noting)
- **Standardised test scores** (if available) — help schools place students in appropriate year levels
- **Reading and maths levels** — a current assessment is often the quickest shortcut

Families who have maintained good records typically find school re-entry far smoother than those who haven''t. The burden of demonstrating grade-level competence is on the homeschooling family, and good records make that straightforward.

## Frequently Asked Questions {#faq}

**What records are homeschoolers required to keep?**
Requirements vary significantly by country, state, and region. In the US, most states require attendance records, and many require annual assessments or portfolio reviews. Always verify your specific jurisdiction''s requirements through your state or national homeschool association.

**How do I track homeschool attendance?**
Record the date, subjects covered, approximate hours, and a brief note about activities. A daily log serves as both attendance record and learning journal. Paper, spreadsheet, or dedicated apps all work well.

**Do homeschoolers need to create transcripts?**
Transcripts become important when a student applies to college or university, seeks employment, or joins the military. Creating transcript-style records from high school onward is strongly recommended.

**How long should homeschool records be kept?**
Keep records for at least 7 years, or until the student reaches adulthood. High school transcripts and graduation documentation should be kept permanently.

**What is a homeschool portfolio and when is it required?**
A portfolio is a curated collection of a student''s work demonstrating learning and progress. Some states require portfolio reviews by a certified evaluator. Even where not required, portfolios are valuable evidence of educational progress.

**Can I use an app or software to keep homeschool records?**
Yes. Several platforms are designed for homeschool record keeping including Homeschool Tracker, Scholaric, and Homeschool Planet. Always maintain a backup across at least two locations.
